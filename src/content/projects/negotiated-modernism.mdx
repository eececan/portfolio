## Where it came from

I took architecture history classes in my senior year of computer engineering at
Bilkent, for fun. Architecture was a hobby of mine and I was genuinely interested
in it.

But one era, one concept, confused me: modernism, the "universal style". It was
too broad. It was not confined to one culture or one country, and there were many
buildings that do not look like each other at all, yet are all considered modern.
You do not see this in Baroque, or in Gothic.

So I started to investigate it building by building, from the bottom up — not to
understand modernism first and then go and look at buildings, but the other way
around.

But how do you investigate each building? Based on what?

Then I decided to compare each building to the ideal modern building: Corbusier's
Five Points. And I wondered how much these *real* buildings deviate from those
ideal principles.

That was the first question. Then another one appeared. If they deviate — and I
know these Five Points are just one man's word — *why* do they deviate? Because of
climate? Because of geography?

So I took buildings by people similar to Corbusier, from his era, built in
different parts of the world.

## What I expected

I expected to find that they deviate on some of the points, and that when they do,
it might be a result of the different climates they were built in.

## How I measured it

I measured each of the Five Points with computer vision techniques.

Some difficulties ran across all of them. The background of a building was often
too colourful or too complex to separate the building from. The photographs were
shot from different angles, so nothing was consistent between one building and
the next. And floor plans have no absolute standard — every one of them is drawn
differently.

### 1 · Ribbon windows

<Figure
	src={glazing}
	alt="Four building facades with hand-marked window regions outlined, labelled with the computed glazing ratio for each."
	caption="Extracted window regions and glazing ratios, measured as a fraction of the whole photograph."
	plate={false}
/>

**How I measured it.** Windows are marked by hand on each facade photograph in
coloured pen. The pipeline pulls the mark out by HSV threshold and computes the
glazed area as a fraction of the facade. I am automating the *area*, not the
identification.

**What was difficult.** The glass reflected so much that the windows could not be
found reliably on their own, which is why I drew them manually in the first place.
Once I was drawing them, two new problems appeared. Windows that sat close
together got merged into one region, which I fixed by decreasing the kernel. And a
reddish pen does not survive a reddish background, so I switched to blue.

One thing I have not resolved: sun protection and shading. Brise-soleil, deep
eaves and louvres all change what a window actually does, and none of that is in a
glazed-area number. I want to circle back to this.

**What I found.** The answer depends on what you divide by, which I did not expect.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Building | Of the whole photo | Of the annotated facade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Villa Savoye | 0.118 | 0.907 |
| Kaufmann Desert House | 0.235 | 0.800 |
| Casa das Canoas | 0.278 | 0.769 |
| Frey House II | 0.304 | 0.608 |

</div>

Measured against the whole photograph, Savoye glazes least and Frey House II most.
Measured against the facade I actually annotated, the order reverses completely —
Savoye becomes the most glazed at 0.907 and Frey House II the least at 0.608.

### 2 · Flat roof

<Figure
	src={roof}
	alt="Five buildings with detected roof edges overlaid in green for horizontal and red for sloped, each labelled with the classifier's decision and confidence."
	caption="Flat-roof classifier: 5/5 correct on this set, including both negative controls. Green edges are horizontal, red are consistently sloped."
	tall
	plate={false}
/>

**How I measured it.** Roof pitch is a three-dimensional property and a single
photograph cannot recover it geometrically, so the decision is made by CLIP
zero-shot classification. A geometric roof slope index runs alongside it as a
one-sided veto for mono-pitch roofs.

**What was difficult.** Telling a flat roof from a terrace. The perspective the
photograph was shot from changes what the roofline even looks like. And Frey House
II has a tilted roof, which left me with a labelling problem before any code ran:
what is the correct answer supposed to be?

**What I found.** 5/5 on this set, including both negative controls.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Building | Flat roof | p(flat) | Slope index | Decided by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Villa Savoye | yes | 0.962 | 0.009 | CLIP |
| Casa das Canoas | yes | 0.695 | 0.075 | CLIP |
| Kaufmann Desert House | yes | 0.860 | 0.150 | CLIP |
| Frey House II | no | 0.486 | 0.967 | mono-pitch veto |
| Suburban house *(control)* | no | 0.256 | 0.000 | CLIP |

</div>

Frey House II came down to a CLIP margin of 0.03, which is effectively a coin
flip. The geometric veto is the only reason it landed on the right side.

### 3 · Open plan

<Figure
	src={planSavoye}
	alt="Villa Savoye first floor plan segmented into eleven coloured room regions."
	caption="Villa Savoye, first floor: 11 segmented rooms. The dominant green region wraps around the ramp core — non-convex, which is exactly what the free plan is supposed to produce."
	plate={false}
/>

**How I measured it.** Floor plans are segmented into rooms by adaptive flood
fill, then scored on three rules. The largest room must dominate the interior
(area > 15%), partition density inside it must be low (< 20%), and it must be
non-convex (convexity < 0.85), which is what separates a flowing space from one
big rectangular one.

**What was difficult.** The flood fill leaked through doors. My first instinct was
to find the doors and close them, and then to hunt for a radius sweet spot that
would bridge gaps without destroying walls. Neither worked.

What worked was morphological bridging — stretching a closing kernel across the
walls so the fill sees an enclosed space, the way a person does. Pixel-level
classifiers had failed here because they identify walls without understanding that
a wall connects to another wall to form a room, so they produce fragments.

Then the bridging itself needed fixing, repeatedly:

- The bridge radius was hardcoded at 60 px, which only worked at one image size. I
  replaced it with adaptive scaling based on the image diagonal.
- Rotonda and Canoas leaked to the outside, because my hand-drawn green boundary
  had tiny gaps at the corners. Applying a closing operation to the green mask
  *before* extracting it glued the corners shut.
- Some `_marked` images were one pixel taller or wider than the original, which
  crashed with an `IndexError`. I forced the marked image to match the base
  image's dimensions.
- Small vestibules were disappearing, so I lowered the minimum area threshold from
  0.5% to 0.1% while keeping the door-bridging radius high.
- Kaufmann's plan is very wide, so scaling the kernel by the diagonal turned it
  into a 30-pixel wrecking ball that erased the thin corridor walls. I capped the
  kernel at 18 px, which preserved the fragile walls of sprawling houses and still
  closed doors.

Scoring had its own failed attempt. I first used continuous weighted scoring, and
it broke on the Rotonda. The Rotonda's central circle has almost no internal
walls, so it scored highly for low wall density and came out looking open. Strict
rule-based scoring fixed it, and convexity turned out to be the rule that does the
work.

<Figure
	src={planRotonda}
	alt="Palladio's Villa Rotonda plan segmented into room regions, showing a symmetrical convex layout."
	caption="The control: Palladio's Villa Rotonda, convexity 0.98, score 30/100 — a near-perfectly convex central hall, correctly classified as not open plan. A scoring rule that passed this would have been worthless."
	plate={false}
/>

**What I found.**

<div class="table-wrap">

| Building | Area ratio | Wall density | Convexity | Score | Open plan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Villa Savoye | 0.34 | 0.094 | 0.71 | 100 | yes |
| Casa das Canoas | 0.21 | 0.186 | 0.57 | 100 | yes |
| Frey House II | 0.50 | 0.149 | 0.79 | 100 | yes |
| Kaufmann Desert House | 0.15 | 0.179 | 0.79 | 70 | yes |
| Villa Rotonda *(control)* | 0.14 | 0.021 | 0.98 | 30 | no |

</div>

All four modern houses pass and the Palladian control fails, which is what the
control was there to check. Kaufmann loses 30 points on the area rule, at 0.15 —
right on the threshold.

### 4 · Free facade

<Figure
	src={facade}
	alt="Villa Savoye facade with the horizontal extent of the window block measured against the extent of glass."
	caption="Free facade test on Villa Savoye: the window block spans 807 px and glass covers all 807 of them, a ratio of 1.00."
	plate={false}
/>

**How I measured it.** The drawn extent ratio: glass pixels divided by the
horizontal distance between the leftmost and rightmost drawn window. It asks how
much of the span occupied by the window group is actually glass.

**What was difficult.** Three earlier metrics failed before this one, each for a
different reason.

*Sum of all window widths, over image width.* Bounding boxes overlap when windows
are drawn at an angle or wrap around a corner, so coverage came out above 100% —
Kaufmann scored 1.274. It also punished buildings where the architect deliberately
left a gap, like a chimney, in the middle of a glass wall.

*Single longest continuous span, over image width.* Too strict. Kaufmann has a
huge glass facade split into two blocks by a central stone chimney, and no single
block passed the 50% threshold, so it came out as a false negative. Canoas failed
too, because its long eaves and the framing of the photograph pushed the image
width far past the actual width of the building.

*Building-width normalisation using Canny.* Exterior photographs have too much
high-frequency noise — leaves, sky, reflections, shadows. Canny could not reliably
find the outer silhouette, so it fell back to the full image dimensions and
defeated the whole point of normalising.

The drawn extent ratio works because it ignores the background and the margins
entirely. It only looks inside the span I drew.

**What I found.** All four pass.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Building | Span | Glass | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Villa Savoye | 807 px | 807 px | 1.00 |
| Casa das Canoas | 508 px | 497 px | 0.98 |
| Frey House II | 724 px | 704 px | 0.97 |
| Kaufmann Desert House | 1200 px | 997 px | 0.83 |

</div>

Kaufmann's missing 17% is the chimney gap — the only interruption in the span.

### 5 · Pilotis

<Figure
	src={pilotis}
	alt="Villa Savoye with eight vertical columns detected and marked in red within a highlighted band at the base of the building."
	caption="Villa Savoye: 8 columns detected, void ratio 0.88. This is the only building on which the detector is validated."
	plate={false}
/>

**How I measured it.** A Hough transform counts vertical supports inside a
horizontal band at the base of the facade, and reports the void ratio of that
band.

**What was difficult.** Manual labelling did not work here, and neither did edge
detection. The void ratio did. What improved it was giving the detector only one
coordinate instead of a full band definition.

The band itself is the real problem. It is defined as a fraction of image height,
and on the other three buildings that fraction lands on terrace, pool or lawn
rather than a ground storey. On Canoas the band comes out 9 px tall — 6% of the
image — which the code now warns about, because a strip that thin cannot contain a
storey.

**What I found.** Savoye: 8 columns, void ratio 0.88. The other three report zero.

That happens to agree with the architecture, since Savoye is the only one of the
four genuinely raised on pilotis — but it is agreement by coincidence, not
measurement. The detector is validated on Savoye alone, and the zeros elsewhere
are absences, not evidence.

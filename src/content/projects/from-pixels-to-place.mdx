## The question

Do architectural styles remain bound to their culture, geography, and era, or do
buildings from distant places and times share a deeper visual DNA?

Architectural history answers this qualitatively, through the trained eye. This
project asks whether the question survives being posed quantitatively — whether
a model never taught architectural periodisation nevertheless organises
buildings in ways a historian would recognise, and what it does when it
disagrees.

## Data

The ECCV2014 architectural styles dataset (Xu et al., ECCV 2014), covering 25
labelled styles from Ancient Egyptian to Deconstructivism. Styles were
stratified to a target of 60 images each — the categories hold 77–80 images, so
60 balances them without discarding much — giving **1,472 images** after
unreadable files were dropped. Balancing matters here: UMAP's neighbourhood
structure is sensitive to density, and an unbalanced corpus would let the
largest style dominate the map.

## Method

1. **Embedding.** Each image is encoded with CLIP ViT-L/14 pretrained on
   LAION-2B, producing L2-normalised 768-dimensional vectors. CLIP is trained on
   general image–text pairs, not on an architectural taxonomy, so any structure
   it recovers is not structure it was taught.
2. **Projection.** UMAP to two dimensions, cosine metric, `n_neighbors=15`,
   `min_dist=0.1`, fixed seed.
3. **Clustering.** HDBSCAN with `min_cluster_size=8`, `min_samples=3`,
   excess-of-mass selection. Density-based clustering means the number of groups
   is discovered rather than specified — important, since fixing it at 25 would
   smuggle the answer into the question.
4. **Comparison.** Cosine similarity between per-style centroids, then Ward
   linkage over that matrix to produce a hierarchy.

A second pass re-ran HDBSCAN *inside* each style's own subspace (PCA to 10
components, `min_cluster_size=6`) to ask whether individual styles are
internally uniform.

## What the map looks like

<Figure
	src={umap}
	alt="UMAP scatter plot of 1,472 building images coloured by architectural style, showing separated regions for ancient, medieval sacred, domestic vernacular, and modern styles."
	caption="UMAP projection of CLIP embeddings, coloured by labelled style. Egyptian and Achaemenid sit isolated on the right; Russian Orthodox forms a tight island at the top; the American domestic styles collapse into a single mass at the bottom centre."
/>

HDBSCAN found **44 clusters** and left 183 points (12.4%) as noise. Three things
stand out.

**Ancient sacred architecture separates cleanly.** Egyptian and Achaemenid
occupy their own region of the map and produce nearly monocultural clusters —
one Egyptian cluster of 34 points is 31 Egyptian, another 14 of 14. These are
the styles with the most distinctive massing and the least overlap with anything
else in the corpus.

**Domestic vernacular collapses.** The single largest cluster holds 316 points
and merges American Foursquare (60), American Craftsman (59), Queen Anne (51),
Tudor Revival (42), Greek Revival (32), Colonial (27) and Georgian (17). The
model is not distinguishing these styles at all. The centroid similarities agree:
American Craftsman and American Foursquare sit at **0.98** cosine similarity,
Queen Anne with either at 0.94–0.95.

This is the most interesting result and also the most uncomfortable one. It is
not obviously a failure of the model. A detached two-storey house photographed
from the street is a strong visual regularity, and it may simply dominate the
stylistic differences that an architectural historian would foreground. Whether
CLIP is missing style or revealing that typology outweighs style is not something
this pipeline can settle on its own.

**Historical lineage shows up where you would expect it.** Byzantine and Russian
Orthodox reach 0.88 similarity, and Byzantine sits equally close to Romanesque —
consistent with the transmission history. Gothic splits across three clusters
with Romanesque leaking into each.

## An unsupervised periodisation

Hierarchical clustering over the centroid matrix recovers a structure nobody
supplied:

<Figure
	src={tree}
	alt="Dendrogram of the 25 architectural styles, splitting first into sacred versus secular, then into domestic and modern branches."
	caption="Ward linkage over centroid cosine distances. The first split separates ancient and medieval sacred architecture from everything else; the second separates domestic and revival styles from the modern movement."
/>

The top-level division is sacred versus secular. Below it, Achaemenid and
Egyptian pair off as the ancient branch; Gothic, Russian Orthodox, Byzantine,
Baroque and Romanesque form the medieval and early-modern sacred branch; the
domestic and revival styles group together; and Bauhaus, International,
Postmodern, Deconstructivism, Art Deco, Art Nouveau, Beaux-Arts and Chicago
School form a modern branch.

No periodisation was given to the model. It arrived at a coarse one from
photographs alone.

## Do styles subdivide?

Running HDBSCAN inside each style's own subspace shows most styles are not
internally uniform. Nearly every style split into two or three sub-clusters.

<Figure

Two are worth naming. **Art Nouveau split geographically**: one sub-cluster is
dominated by Nancy, the other by Riga and Lviv — the French and Eastern European
schools separated without any location information being supplied. **Achaemenid
split by site**, isolating Persepolis from the Naqsh-e Rostam rock-cut tombs.

## Limitations

**Clustering ran on the 2-D projection, not the embeddings.** HDBSCAN was fitted
to UMAP output rather than to the 768-dimensional vectors. This is common
practice and it makes the clusters match the figure, but UMAP does not preserve
density faithfully, so the cluster count is partly an artefact of the projection.
Clustering the full embeddings and comparing would be the first thing to fix.

**The corpus is Western.** All 25 styles are European or American. Any claim
about similarity "across cultures" is therefore a claim about variation within
one broad tradition. Adding Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic, Khmer, Mayan and
Mesopotamian architecture is the obvious extension and would change what the
top-level split means.

**Photographic convention is a confound.** The corpus is Wikimedia-derived, and
conventions differ systematically by category — sacred buildings photographed
frontally and at distance, houses from the street corner. Some of what reads as
stylistic similarity is similarity of the photograph, not the building.

**CLIP's training data is not neutral.** LAION-2B is heavily Western-weighted,
so the model's sensitivity is unevenly distributed across exactly the axis the
project is asking about.

## What's next

Cluster the full 768-dimensional embeddings and treat the UMAP purely as a
visualisation. Extend the corpus beyond Western styles. Then test the typology
confound directly, by controlling for viewing angle and building function to see
how much of the domestic-style collapse survives.
